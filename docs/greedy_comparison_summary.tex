\begin{abstract}
We build a reduced \emph{positive cone}
$K=\operatorname{span}_+\{w_1,\dots,w_R\}$ approximating a set of nonnegative
snapshots, and compare three greedy constructions --- Cone-Projected Greedy
(\CPG), Angular-Defect Greedy (\ADG), modified \CPG\ (\mCPG) --- on a
low-dimensional \emph{lambda} dataset and a high-dimensional \emph{physics}
contact dataset. The note is organised as the \emph{evolution} of the method: a
fair baseline comparison exposes two weaknesses of the raw greedy cones (poor
worst-case relative accuracy when snapshot magnitudes vary, and severe
ill-conditioning from near-duplicate generators); we then add two ingredients to
\ADG\ --- \emph{per-snapshot normalisation} and an \emph{angular redundancy}
control $\eang$ --- and fold them into a \textbf{standard \ADG}. At equal cone
size the standard \ADG\ is $3$--$5\times$ more accurate than \CPG/\mCPG\ on the
wide-magnitude physics data, and on the well-scaled lambda data it turns an
otherwise catastrophic condition number ($\sim\!10^{10}$) into $\sim\!10^{1}$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Algorithms.}
All three methods grow a positive cone one generator (or, for \ADG, one batch)
at a time and project each snapshot $x$ onto the current cone by non-negative
least squares (NNLS), $\Pi_K(x)=\arg\min_{c\ge0}\|Ac-x\|_2$ with $A$ the
generators.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \CPG\ adds the snapshot of largest \emph{absolute} residual
        $\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2$.
  \item \ADG\ adds the snapshot of largest \emph{angle}
        $\theta_K(x)=\arcsin\!\big(\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2/\|x\|_2\big)$ to the cone ---
        a \emph{scale-invariant} novelty measure, so its selection is intrinsically
        insensitive to snapshot magnitude.
  \item \mCPG\ selects like \CPG\ but stores a shifted, re-normalised generator
        $\nu=(x-\Pi_K(x))/\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2$, carrying only the direction new to the
        cone.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metric.}
We report the \emph{per-snapshot relative residual}
$\rho(x)=\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2/\|x\|_2$, its worst case $\rho_{\max}$ and mean
$\bar\rho$ over the dataset, evaluated on the \emph{original, physical-scale}
snapshots for every method, plus the basis condition number
$\kappa_2=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$.

wins.

\paragraph{Two weaknesses.}
(i) \textbf{Accuracy on wide-magnitude data.} On physics, \CPG/\mCPG\ rank
candidates by \emph{absolute} residual, so they chase a few high-magnitude
snapshots and leave the small-magnitude ones badly resolved
($\rho_{\max}\approx0.8$); \ADG's angle criterion already does better ($0.35$),
but not enough. (ii) \textbf{Conditioning.} On lambda the snapshots are nearly
collinear, so raw-snapshot cones (\CPG, \ADG) accumulate near-parallel generators
and become singular ($\kappa_2\approx5\times10^{10}$). The next two ideas target
these directly, on \ADG.

\section{Idea 1 --- per-snapshot normalisation}

\ADG's \emph{selection} is scale-invariant, but its \emph{stopping} rule still
compares the absolute residual to a single global scale $\varepsilon\max_x\|x\|$.
On wide-magnitude data this lets a small snapshot leave the ``unresolved'' set
while still $50\%$+ wrong in relative terms. Measuring the error relative to each
snapshot's own norm (dividing $x$ by $\|x\|$ before the stopping check, while
still storing physical-scale generators) makes the criterion a genuine
per-snapshot relative one. It is applied to \ADG\ \emph{only}; \CPG/\mCPG\ have
no such option and stay on the raw snapshots.

Figure~\ref{fig:norm-tail} shows the effect on physics: the raw methods leave a
broad plateau of small-magnitude snapshots stuck near $10^{-2}$ relative error,
while normalised \ADG\ drives them to machine precision. At $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$
normalised \ADG\ reaches $\rho_{\max}=0.143$ (mean $3.7\times10^{-3}$) versus
$0.35$ for raw \ADG\ and $0.81$ for \CPG/\mCPG. On lambda (uniform norms)
normalisation is inert.

\section{Idea 2 --- angular redundancy control}

Normalisation improves accuracy but not conditioning or cone size. On nearly
collinear data \ADG\ keeps adding generators that point almost the same way,
inflating $R$ and $\kappa_2$. The redundancy control adds a second tolerance
$\eang$: a candidate is admitted only if its pairwise angle to \emph{every}
existing generator exceeds $\arcsin\eang$, and enrichment stops once every
remaining unresolved snapshot is redundant. No two generators can then lie within
$\arcsin\eang$ of each other, so $R$ and $\kappa_2$ shrink at a controlled
accuracy cost (Table~\ref{tab:redund}). It can only \emph{keep or diminish} $R$;
$\eang=0$ recovers plain \ADG.

\section{Idea 3 --- angular batching (a cost lever)}

A third, optional knob $\eang^{\mathrm{batch}}$ admits, per round, a
\emph{pairwise-separated batch} of the near-maximal-angle candidates instead of
one. It leaves $R$ and accuracy essentially unchanged but cuts the number of
greedy rounds several-fold (e.g.\ physics $9\to5$, a smooth synthetic set
$41\to4$), which matters when the per-round NNLS projection dominates cost. It is
orthogonal to the two ideas above and off by default.

\section{The standard ADG and a fair comparison}

We define the \textbf{standard \ADG} as plain \ADG\ with per-snapshot
normalisation on and the redundancy control $\eang$ as a single knob (default
$0.05$). To compare it fairly against \CPG/\mCPG\ we let the standard \ADG\ choose
the cone size $R$ (via $\eang$), then fit \CPG\ and \mCPG\ to the \emph{same} $R$
and compare --- a matched-cone-size head-to-head
(Figures~\ref{fig:matched-physics}, \ref{fig:matched-lambda};
Table~\ref{tab:matched}).

\paragraph{Reading the comparison.}
On \textbf{physics} the standard \ADG\ dominates: at any cone size its worst-case
relative error is $\approx0.14$ while \CPG/\mCPG\ sit at $0.52$--$0.84$, because
only \ADG\ (through normalisation) attends to the small-magnitude snapshots. On
\textbf{lambda} the three methods are close on accuracy at matched $R$; \mCPG\ is
the best accuracy-per-component method, while the standard \ADG's contribution is
the redundancy knob that yields a compact, well-conditioned cone
(Table~\ref{tab:redund}).

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Baseline.} Raw greedy cones have two failure modes: poor
        worst-case relative accuracy when snapshot magnitudes vary (\CPG/\mCPG\ on
        physics), and near-singular bases from redundant generators (\CPG/\ADG\ on
        lambda).
  \item \textbf{Normalisation} fixes the first for \ADG: on physics it cuts the
        worst-case relative error from $0.35$ (raw \ADG) / $0.81$ (\CPG/\mCPG) to
        $0.14$; it is inert on uniform-magnitude data.
  \item \textbf{Angular redundancy} fixes the second: it shrinks $R$ and turns the
        lambda cone's $\kappa_2\approx5\times10^{10}$ into $\sim\!10^{2}$
        ($\sim\!10^{1}$ at $R=2$), for a modest accuracy cost, and reduces $R$ for
        free on physics.
  \item \textbf{Standard \ADG} (normalisation $+$ redundancy) is best on
        wide-magnitude data at every matched cone size ($3$--$5\times$ lower
        $\rho_{\max}$ than \CPG/\mCPG); on well-scaled data \mCPG\ leads on accuracy
        while the standard \ADG\ offers a compact, well-conditioned alternative.
        \emph{Guidance:} use the standard \ADG\ for data with a wide spread of
        snapshot magnitudes or when a small, well-conditioned cone matters; use
        \mCPG\ for well-scaled data where error-per-component is paramount; add the
        batching knob when the round count is the bottleneck.
\end{itemize}
