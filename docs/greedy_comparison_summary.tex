\begin{abstract}
We build a reduced \emph{positive cone}
$K=\operatorname{span}_+\{w_1,\dots,w_R\}$ approximating a set of nonnegative
snapshots, and compare three greedy constructions --- Cone-Projected Greedy
(\CPG), Angular-Defect Greedy (\ADG), modified \CPG\ (\mCPG) --- on a
low-dimensional \emph{lambda} dataset and a high-dimensional \emph{physics}
contact dataset. The note is organised as the \emph{evolution} of the method: a
fair baseline comparison exposes two weaknesses of the raw greedy cones (poor
worst-case relative accuracy when snapshot magnitudes vary, and severe
ill-conditioning from near-duplicate generators); we then add one ingredient to
\ADG\ --- \emph{per-snapshot normalisation} --- and call the result the
\textbf{standard \ADG}. Geometrically this ingredient is a single move
(Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry}): it sends every snapshot to the unit sphere and turns
the positive cone into a spherical region, so novelty becomes a geodesic
distance and the whole method is read intrinsically, on directions alone. It
addresses the first weakness and not the second. At
equal cone size the standard \ADG\ is $15$--$28\times$ more accurate than
\CPG/\mCPG\ on the wide-magnitude physics data (magnitude ratio $604$); on the
well-scaled lambda data it selects the same generators as \CPG, inherits the same
$\kappa_2\sim10^{10}$, and \mCPG\ dominates both. Conditioning is therefore
\mCPG's contribution, not \ADG's. We then re-test the story on four independent
contact-force datasets (membrane obstacle, and three Hertz-contact cases), in
their dual $W$-inner product and with held-out parameters. They confirm the
magnitude-ratio thesis on data it was not developed on, and force two
refinements: normalisation is \emph{harmful}, not merely inert, on well-scaled
data --- including on lambda, where it was first called inert; and \CPG\ and
\ADG\ are \emph{not} the same algorithm, despite selecting identically on every
dataset the method was developed on.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Algorithms.}
All three methods grow a positive cone one generator at a time (\ADG\ admits
every snapshot tied at the maximal angle, so a round may add more than one)
and project each snapshot $x$ onto the current cone by non-negative
least squares (NNLS), $\Pi_K(x)=\arg\min_{c\ge0}\|Ac-x\|_2$ with $A$ the
generators.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \CPG\ adds the snapshot of largest \emph{absolute} residual
        $\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2$.
  \item \ADG\ adds the snapshot of largest \emph{angle}
        $\theta_K(x)=\arcsin\!\big(\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2/\|x\|_2\big)$ to the cone ---
        a \emph{scale-invariant} novelty measure, so its selection is intrinsically
        insensitive to snapshot magnitude. A round admits \emph{every} snapshot tied
        at the maximal angle $\theta_{\max}$, and per-snapshot normalisation
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry}) is its only remaining knob; the \textbf{standard
        \ADG} is \ADG\ with that knob on.
  \item \mCPG\ selects like \CPG\ but stores a shifted, re-normalised generator
        $\nu=(x-\Pi_K(x))/\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2$, carrying only the direction new to the
        cone.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metric.}
We report the \emph{per-snapshot relative residual}
$\rho(x)=\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2/\|x\|_2$, its worst case $\rho_{\max}$ and mean
$\bar\rho$ over the dataset, evaluated on the \emph{original, physical-scale}
snapshots for every method, plus the basis condition number
$\kappa_2=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$.

\paragraph{Datasets.}
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists the six datasets. The magnitude ratio
$\max_x\|x\|_2/\min_x\|x\|_2$ is the single quantity that predicts which method
wins.

\section{The standard ADG on the unit sphere}
\label{sec:geometry}

The single ingredient that turns \ADG\ into the \emph{standard} \ADG\ is
per-snapshot normalisation. Algebraically it is small: divide each snapshot by
its own norm before the stopping/unresolved check, while still storing
\emph{physical-scale} generators, so $\varepsilon$ becomes a genuine per-snapshot
relative tolerance rather than the absolute floor $\varepsilon\max_x\|x\|$ shared
across the dataset. It is applied to \ADG\ only; \CPG/\mCPG\ have no such option
and stay on the raw snapshots. It moves the stopping test, never the argmax:
because the angle $\theta_K$ is scale-invariant, normalisation cannot change
\emph{which} generator a round selects, only how many rounds run before every
snapshot counts as resolved.

Geometrically that small move is one clean picture, and it is the whole of what
``standard'' means here (Figs.~\ref{fig:sphere-2d}--\ref{fig:sphere-3d}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Snapshots live on the unit sphere.} Normalising
        $x\mapsto \hat x=x/\|x\|$ sends every nonzero snapshot to a point of the
        unit sphere $S^{d-1}$: magnitude is discarded and only direction
        survives. The wide-magnitude cloud of the left panels collapses onto the
        sphere on the right.
  \item \textbf{The cone becomes a spherical region.} The positive cone
        $K=\operatorname{span}_+\{w_1,\dots,w_R\}$ is scale-invariant
        ($x\in K\iff\hat x\in K$), so it is fully described by its trace
        $\widehat A(K)=K\cap S^{d-1}$ --- an \emph{arc} in $\mathbb{R}^2$
        (Fig.~\ref{fig:sphere-2d}, right), a geodesically convex \emph{spherical
        triangle}/cap in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sphere-3d}, right). The
        generator directions $\hat w_i$ are its vertices and $\widehat A(K)$ is
        their geodesic convex hull; its area (the solid angle read out in
        Fig.~\ref{fig:sphere-3d}, $0.36$~sr, $2.9\%$ of the sphere) is the
        fraction of directions the cone can represent.
  \item \textbf{Novelty is a geodesic distance.} For a snapshot direction
        $\hat x$, the selection score
        $\theta_K(x)=\arcsin(\|x-\Pi_K(x)\|_2/\|x\|_2)$ is exactly the
        great-circle angle from $\hat x$ to $\widehat A(K)$ (the residual
        $x-\Pi_K(x)$ is orthogonal to $\Pi_K(x)$, so the triangle
        $0,\Pi_K(x),x$ is right-angled). \ADG\ therefore adds the snapshot whose
        direction is geodesically \emph{farthest} from the current region --- it
        patches the widest angular gap in the sphere's coverage. Scale-invariance
        is manifest: two snapshots on one ray are the same point of $S^{d-1}$ and
        are indistinguishable to the rule.
  \item \textbf{Standard \ADG\ is the whole analysis done on the sphere.} Raw
        \ADG\ scores its stopping residual against one global scale
        $\varepsilon\max_x\|x\|$, so a small-magnitude snapshot can drop below
        that absolute floor while its \emph{direction} is still far from
        $\widehat A(K)$. Normalisation deletes the magnitude axis: the stopping
        test becomes ``is every snapshot direction within the target angular
        tolerance of $\widehat A(K)$?'', the intrinsic question the sphere makes
        obvious. On wide-magnitude data (physics, ratio $604$) this rescues the
        small snapshots the floor hides; on well-scaled data (ratio $\approx1$)
        every point already sits in a thin band on the sphere, so normalisation
        changes only the bookkeeping (Sec.~\ref{sec:norm}).
  \item \textbf{The certificate is an angular defect.} The worst residual after
        a round is bounded by $\max(\varepsilon C,\,C\sin\theta_{\max})$ with
        $C=\max_x\|x\|$ and $\theta_{\max}$ the largest remaining geodesic gap ---
        the \emph{angular defect} that names the method. The bound is checked
        against the recorded convergence history at every round
        (\texttt{verify\_angular\_defect\_certificate}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Two weaknesses.}
(i) \textbf{Accuracy on wide-magnitude data.} On physics, \CPG/\mCPG\ rank
candidates by \emph{absolute} residual, so they chase a few high-magnitude
snapshots and leave the small-magnitude ones badly resolved
($\rho_{\max}\approx0.8$); \ADG's angle criterion already does better ($0.35$),
but not enough. (ii) \textbf{Conditioning.} On lambda the snapshots are nearly
collinear, so raw-snapshot cones (\CPG, \ADG) accumulate near-parallel generators
and become singular ($\kappa_2\approx5\times10^{10}$). Only the first of these is
an \ADG\ problem with an \ADG\ fix, and Sec.~\ref{sec:norm} gives it. The second
is structural: any method that stores raw snapshots as generators inherits it, so
it is answered by \mCPG's shifted, re-normalised generators rather than by
anything \ADG\ can do to its selection rule.

\section{What normalisation buys, and what it costs}
\label{sec:norm}

Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry} defined the operation and its geometry; this section is
what it does to the numbers, on the two datasets it was developed on. The rule
of thumb is the magnitude ratio: normalisation rescues masked small-magnitude
snapshots when the ratio is large, and merely demands more generators when it is
near $1$.

Figure~\ref{fig:norm-tail} shows the effect on physics: the raw methods leave a
broad plateau of small-magnitude snapshots stuck near $10^{-2}$ relative error,
while normalised \ADG\ drives them to machine precision. At $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$
normalised \ADG\ reaches $\rho_{\max}=0.143$ (mean $3.7\times10^{-3}$) versus
$0.35$ for raw \ADG\ and $0.81$ for \CPG/\mCPG.

On lambda (magnitude ratio $1.13$) it is \emph{not} free, and calling it ``inert''
there --- as an earlier version of this note did --- understates the cost: it
inflates $R$ from $23$ to $26$ and $\kappa_2$ from $5.43\times10^{10}$ to
$1.38\times10^{11}$, buying a $\rho_{\max}$ of $9.52\times10^{-3}$ against
$1.10\times10^{-2}$ --- a $13\%$ accuracy gain for $13\%$ more generators and
$2.5\times$ worse conditioning. That is the same pattern the contact-force sets
show (Sec.~\ref{sec:contact}), so it is not a quirk of those datasets: whenever
the magnitude ratio is near $1$ there is no masked small-magnitude snapshot to
rescue, and the stricter criterion only demands more generators. Normalisation
earns its keep on physics (ratio $604$), not here.

\section{Fair comparison at matched cone size}

\textbf{This is the fair comparison, and the one to read.} A method is only worth
comparing to another at the \emph{same budget}: equal cone size $R$, i.e.\ equal
number of generators. Because normalisation moves \ADG's stopping test but never
its argmax (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry}), a common $\varepsilon$ stops the three
methods at different $R$, so their own stopping points measure the tolerance as
much as the method (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). We remove that confound by walking
each basis prefix $K_1,\dots,K_R$ and comparing at \emph{matched} $R$
(Table~\ref{tab:matched}); all three greedies are nested, so a prefix of length
$n$ is exactly the cone that method would have produced had $\varepsilon$ stopped
it at $n$. Every conclusion in this note rests on these equal-$R$ numbers, not on
the equal-$\varepsilon$ baseline. The rows below were regenerated from the current
pipelines and match to all reported digits.

\paragraph{Reading the comparison.}
On \textbf{physics} the standard \ADG\ dominates on accuracy at every budget: its
worst-case relative error is $15$--$28\times$ lower than \CPG/\mCPG\ ($1.87\times
10^{-2}$ versus $5.20\times10^{-1}$ at $R=30$), because only \ADG, through
normalisation, attends to the small-magnitude snapshots the other two leave
$50\%$ wrong. Note what it does \emph{not} buy: \ADG's cone is no better
conditioned than \CPG's, and at small $R$ it is an order of magnitude
\emph{worse} ($1.3\times10^3$ against $1.0\times10^2$ at $R=5$). Accuracy on
wide-magnitude data is the whole of the claim.

On \textbf{lambda} the standard \ADG\ and \CPG\ are indistinguishable --- same
$\rho_{\max}$ at every $R$, same $\kappa_2$ to within rounding --- because with a
magnitude ratio of $1.13$ there is no masked snapshot for the angle criterion to
rescue, and the two selection rules agree. Both become numerically singular past
$R\approx21$. \mCPG\ is the only method that is both more accurate
($6.55\times10^{-3}$ at $R=30$, an order of magnitude better) and better
conditioned. On well-scaled data \ADG\ has nothing to add over \CPG, and the
right choice is \mCPG.

\section{Confirmation on four contact-force datasets}
\label{sec:contact}

The three ideas above were developed on \emph{lambda} and \emph{physics}. We now
test them on four independent datasets --- the membrane-obstacle and Hertz-contact
test cases of Niakh, Drouet, Ehrlacher \& Ern (ESAIM:M2AN 2022), plus two further
reconstructions of the Hertz physics, cases~(a) and~(b) --- where
$\lambda(\mu)\ge0$ \emph{is} the physical contact force. Sections~\ref{sec:contact}
--\ref{sec:floor} report the first two, on which the analysis was done;
Sec.~\ref{sec:contact-new} adds cases~(a) and~(b). Together they confirm the note's
central thesis and force \emph{three} refinements --- the third, from case~(a),
retracting a claim the other three datasets had made look like a law.

\paragraph{What is different here.}
Three things depart from the lambda and physics analysis above, and each matters
for reading the numbers.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A non-Euclidean dual inner product.} Errors, projections and
        angles are measured in $\|x\|_W=\sqrt{x^\top W x}$ with $W$ the dual mass
        matrix shipped with each dataset, not in $\|\cdot\|_2$. We factor
        $W=U^\top U$ and run the \emph{unmodified} Euclidean algorithms on
        $U$-transformed snapshots: $\|x\|_W=\|Ux\|_2$ and $U(Ac)=(UA)c$ with the
        same $c\ge0$, so the transformed runs \emph{are} the $W$-norm greedies.
        The lone exception is \mCPG's cone-shift constraint
        $\Upsilon\le\theta$, which is elementwise and \emph{not} preserved by
        $U$; \mCPG\ is given $U^{-1}$ so that bound is imposed in physical
        coordinates.
  \item \textbf{Held-out testing.} Unlike Tables~\ref{tab:baseline}
        and~\ref{tab:matched}, which score the whole dataset, these runs fit on a
        training set and report a held-out $\rho_{\max}$ at unseen $\mu$. Hertz
        holds out interior $R_2$ values ($65/16$); the membrane holds out the
        strict interior of its $5\times5\times5$ grid ($98/27$), since its
        $\mu_1$ takes only $5$ distinct values and a split on it would be
        degenerate. This turns out to change the conclusion (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).
  \item \textbf{Feasible tolerances.} $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$ is unreachable on the
        membrane: POD needs $R=97/98$ to resolve the training set at all, so
        every method simply exhausts it ($R=P$). The membrane is therefore run at
        $\varepsilon=0.25$. Hertz uses $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$ as before.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Normalisation on new data: not merely inert --- harmful}

Sec.~\ref{sec:norm} found normalisation ``inert'' on uniform-magnitude data. On these sets it is
worse than inert: at ratios of $1.16$/$1.30$ there is no masked small-magnitude
snapshot for it to rescue, so the stricter per-snapshot criterion only demands more
generators for no return. On Hertz it takes \ADG\ from $R=38$ to $R=42$ and
$\kappa_2$ from $5.5\times10^{9}$ to $4.6\times10^{11}$; on the membrane from
$R=56$ to $R=82$. Held-out error is unchanged to three digits in both
(\texttt{adg\_variants.csv}). Normalisation here costs $11\%$ more generators and
two orders of magnitude of conditioning, and returns nothing.

\emph{Refinement.} Normalisation should be read as conditional on the magnitude
ratio, not as free: switch it on for wide-magnitude data (physics, ratio $604$),
off otherwise. Since the standard \ADG\ is normalisation, this says the standard
\ADG\ is itself conditional --- it is the right method only in the wide-magnitude
regime.

\subsection{What only held-out testing reveals: a generalisation floor}
\label{sec:floor}

Every lambda and physics table above scores the training data (there, the whole
dataset). On Hertz that would have hidden
the most important effect on these datasets: \textbf{the held-out error floors}.
\CPG/\ADG's test $\rho_{\max}$ is pinned at $6.280\times10^{-2}$ from $R=12$ all the
way to $R=38$ --- the last $26$ generators improve training error $4\times$ and
generalisation by \emph{nothing}. \mCPG\ is the only method that keeps improving out
of sample ($6.10\times10^{-2}$ at $R{=}12$, $6.04\times10^{-2}$ at $R{=}16$,
$4.82\times10^{-2}$ at $R{=}25$).

The floor is the practical result of this section. It says the useful budget on
Hertz is $R\approx12$, and that everything past it is training-set decoration:
$\varepsilon$ is being asked for an accuracy the data cannot generalise to. It
also explains why a smaller cone can look free on this dataset --- past the floor,
generators cost nothing observable to discard, so any procedure that stops early
appears to lose nothing while in fact converging to a training error it never
reaches out of sample.

\paragraph{Why the membrane is hard.}
Even a cone built from \emph{all} $98$ training snapshots leaves a held-out
$\rho_{\max}\approx0.355$, against $\approx0.12$ for an unconstrained POD subspace
of the same data. Nonnegative combinations can only add mass, never cancel it, so a
reaction bump at an unseen $(\mu_2,\mu_3)$ is unreachable from bumps centred
elsewhere. This is the moving-support regime, and no knob studied here addresses
it.

\subsection{Two further Hertz cases: where \texorpdfstring{$\CPG\equiv\ADG$}{CPG=ADG} breaks}
\label{sec:contact-new}

Two additional reconstructions of the Hertz physics sharpen the picture. Both put
$\lambda$ on the same reference arc ($51$ uniform nodes on $[-1,1]$) but drive it
differently: \textbf{case~(a)} sweeps the \emph{imposed displacement}
$\mu=d\in[0.15,0.45]$ ($31$ snapshots, held out $25/6$), \textbf{case~(b)} the
\emph{geometry} $\mu=R_2\in[0.905,1.125]$ ($23$ snapshots, $18/5$). Pressing harder
in~(a) both scales the semi-ellipse up and widens its support, which is why~(a) is
the only set here with a non-trivial magnitude ratio ($3.93$,
Table~\ref{tab:datasets}); (b)'s narrow range about $R_2\approx1$ barely moves the
profile (ratio $1.09$).

\paragraph{A caveat on the metric.}
Unlike the other two sets, neither ships a $W$ matrix. Both live on a $1$-D contact
arc, so $W$ is rebuilt as the $P_1$ arc mass matrix of the node abscissas --- the
same construction that produced the Hertz set's own $W$. Without it the ``$W$-norm''
would silently degrade to a mesh-dependent nodal norm, so every number below rests
on that reconstruction.

\paragraph{They do not discriminate on accuracy.}
Both are far lower-rank than Hertz (numerical rank $14$ and $8$; POD reaches
$1.8\times10^{-6}$ by $R=4$ on~(b)). At $\varepsilon=10^{-2}$ all three methods hit
the tolerance at $R=7$~(a) / $R=3$~(b) with \emph{identical} errors, so
Table~\ref{tab:contact-new} reports the discriminating $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$.
Conditioning is the only axis that separates the methods --- and \mCPG\ wins it by
one to two orders, as on Hertz. These two sets are best read as a conditioning
benchmark, not as evidence about accuracy.

\paragraph{Refinement 3: \CPG\ and \ADG\ are not the same algorithm.}
Table~\ref{tab:contact-baseline} and Table~\ref{tab:contact-matched} record
\CPG\ and \ADG\ as bit-identical, and lambda said the same; it is tempting to read
that as a law. It is not. On case~(a) at $\varepsilon=10^{-3}$ they again coincide
--- both select $\{0,3,6,10,15,20,25,30\}$, differing only in order --- but at
$\varepsilon=0.05$ they \emph{diverge, and \ADG\ loses}:

The angular criterion prefers node $15$ over node $10$ and that is the wrong call:
$2.1\times$ worse held-out error at the same budget, and worse conditioning too. So
the identity is a property of these particular datasets, not of the two
algorithms --- it holds while $\varepsilon$ is tight enough that both are forced
onto essentially the whole training set, and breaks as soon as $\varepsilon$ is
loose enough for the choice to bite. Where they differ, plain \ADG\ is the one that
loses. Across all six datasets and every $\varepsilon$ tested, plain \ADG\ never
once beats \CPG: it ties or it loses.

\paragraph{Normalisation: a lower bound on where it pays.}
Case~(a)'s ratio of $3.93$ is the first intermediate sample between the well-scaled
sets ($1.1$--$1.3$) and physics ($604$). Normalisation is still \emph{harmful}
there: $R$ goes $8\to9$ and $\kappa_2$ $1.8\times10^{3}\to1.6\times10^{5}$ at
unchanged held-out error. A ratio near $4$ is therefore not enough to make the
per-snapshot criterion pay; the threshold lies well above it, closer to physics'
regime than to these.

\paragraph{The floor reappears.}
Case~(a) reproduces Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}'s effect: held-out $\rho_{\max}$ is pinned
at $1.438\times10^{-2}$ from $R=7$ onward --- for \CPG\ and \mCPG\ alike, and for
every \ADG\ variant that runs as far as $R=11$ --- while training error keeps
falling. That the floor is common to all methods and variants on a second,
independently generated dataset supports reading it as a property of the
interpolation split rather than of any one greedy.

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Geometry.} The standard \ADG\ is \ADG\ read on the unit sphere
        (Sec.~\ref{sec:geometry}): normalisation sends snapshots to $S^{d-1}$ and
        the cone to a spherical region $\widehat A(K)$, making novelty a geodesic
        distance and the stopping test an intrinsic ``every direction covered to
        tolerance'' check. This is exactly why it helps when magnitudes vary and
        is inert when they do not.
  \item \textbf{Baseline.} Raw greedy cones have two failure modes: poor
        worst-case relative accuracy when snapshot magnitudes vary (\CPG/\mCPG\ on
        physics), and near-singular bases from near-parallel generators
        (\CPG/\ADG\ on lambda).
  \item \textbf{Normalisation} fixes the first for \ADG, and defines the
        \textbf{standard \ADG}: on physics it cuts the worst-case relative error
        from $0.52$ (\CPG/\mCPG) to $0.019$ at a matched $R=30$, a $28\times$
        margin that holds at every budget.
  \item \textbf{Nothing in \ADG\ fixes the second.} The conditioning failure is
        structural --- any cone that stores raw snapshots as generators inherits
        it --- so it is \mCPG's shifted, re-normalised generators that answer it,
        not \ADG. On lambda the standard \ADG\ is numerically singular at large
        $R$ exactly like \CPG\ ($\kappa_2\approx5\times10^{11}$ at $R=30$), while
        \mCPG\ is both an order of magnitude more accurate and better conditioned.
  \item \textbf{Standard \ADG} is therefore a specialist, not a default: best on
        wide-magnitude data at every matched cone size, and indistinguishable from
        \CPG\ everywhere else. \emph{Guidance:} use the standard \ADG\ when
        snapshot magnitudes span orders of magnitude; use \mCPG\ otherwise --- on
        well-scaled data it leads on both accuracy and conditioning, and \ADG\ adds
        nothing over \CPG.
  \item \textbf{Confirmed, with two refinements} (Sec.~\ref{sec:contact}). Four
        independent contact-force datasets reproduce the lambda pattern exactly
        --- magnitude ratio $\approx1.2$, \CPG/\ADG\ identical and singular
        ($\kappa_2=5.5\times10^{9}$), \mCPG\ compact and well-conditioned --- so
        the magnitude ratio predicts the winner on data the method was not tuned
        on. But: (i) normalisation is \emph{harmful} rather than inert when the
        ratio is near $1$ ($R$: $38\to42$, $\kappa_2$: $5.5\times10^{9}\to
        4.6\times10^{11}$ on Hertz), so the standard \ADG\ is itself conditional
        on the regime;
        (ii) \textbf{$\CPG\equiv\ADG$ is not a law} (Sec.~\ref{sec:contact-new}).
        It holds on lambda, Hertz and Hertz~(b), but breaks on Hertz~(a) at
        $\varepsilon=0.05$, where \ADG\ makes a different --- and $2.1\times$
        worse --- held-out choice at the same $R=4$. Plain \ADG\ ties or loses to
        \CPG\ on every dataset tested; it never wins.
  \item \textbf{Where normalisation starts to pay.} Hertz~(a) is the only sample
        at an intermediate magnitude ratio ($3.93$), and normalisation is still
        harmful there ($\kappa_2$: $1.8\times10^{3}\to1.6\times10^{5}$). The
        ratio at which the per-snapshot criterion begins to earn its keep is thus
        bounded well above $4$ --- the physics set ($604$) remains the only
        evidence that it ever does.
  \item \textbf{Train-only metrics hide the main effect.} With held-out
        parameters, \CPG/\ADG's test error is flat from $R=12$ to $R=38$ on Hertz:
        $26$ generators improve training error $4\times$ and generalisation not at
        all. Only \mCPG\ keeps improving out of sample. Any future knob should be
        judged against held-out error at matched $R$, not training error at its
        own stopping point.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A note on the timing columns.}
The \texttt{elapsed\_seconds} column of \texttt{comparison\_metrics.csv} was
long unusable: the three methods are fitted in a fixed order, and the first one
run absorbed the cost of spawning a \texttt{joblib} worker pool
($\approx1.6$\,s), which made \CPG\ look uniformly slower than it is. The
parallelism turned out to be a pessimisation on every workload here --- each
per-row NNLS solve takes microseconds while joblib pickles the basis matrix per
row --- so it was removed; serial is $5$--$260\times$ faster with bit-identical
results. The column is now a fair comparison, and it says something the old
numbers hid: \mCPG\ is the \emph{slowest} of the three (membrane, $\varepsilon=0.25$:
\CPG\ $0.68$\,s, \ADG\ $0.18$\,s, \mCPG\ $5.51$\,s), because its constrained
cone-shift QP runs once per generator. That cost is the price of the
conditioning win in Table~\ref{tab:contact-baseline}.
